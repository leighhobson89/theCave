# Ideas — the hidden truth of Black Pine

**Status: mostly still a design document.** It started as a pure
proposal — the canonical story as you've described it, written out in
full, cross-checked line-by-line against everything already shipped so
nothing here contradicts it, plus a menu of new evidence you could
produce to let a player actually *reconstruct* this rather than be told
it. **Tier 1 and Tier 2 item 4 of §4 have since actually been built** —
look for the ✅ marks. Everything else below is still just a suggestion
to accept, reject or rewrite, not a spec.

The single most important thing this document argues is: **`story_en.md`
does not need to change at all.** Everything below sits as a hidden layer
underneath the existing background story, which — reread with this in mind
— turns out to already foreshadow almost all of it. That's covered in
detail in §2.

---

## 1. The hidden story

### Before that morning

Tony had heard rumours for a while that the old Black Pine seams weren't
as "exhausted" as the 1851 closure poster claimed — that there was still
silver in the mine, if a man knew where to dig and wasn't afraid of doing
it without permission. He'd said nothing to anyone; a farmhand's wage
doesn't leave much room for patience, and he'd been waiting for a reason
to go looking.

The night the boys made their plan in their bedroom, Tony — sleeping in
the barn loft, as farmhands did — caught enough of it through the wall to
know they'd found *something* out in the forest and meant to go back to
it at first light. He didn't know what. He didn't need to. Two farm boys
sneaking off before dawn, refusing to say where, was enough. Later that
night he let himself into the tool shed for a length of rope and a knife
of his own, and forgot to lock it behind him — which is why, the next
morning, Andrew found the door open and assumed the household simply
hadn't locked it. Nobody thought to ask why.

### The morning

Tony wasn't with Simon at the milking shed at seven, which is why Andrew's
question — "Where's Tony?" — only gets "About somewhere" for an answer. He
was already gone, keeping his distance behind the boys on the forest path,
letting them lead him to wherever it was they were so determined to reach.

### At the cave

The stranger who spoke to the boys on the path the day before — the one
who knew the "bottomless hole" by reputation, who let slip he hadn't heard
the old mine mentioned "in years," who lingered a moment too long before
walking away — is proposed here as the same man waiting at the cave the
next morning. Not a coincidence, and not a new character: he'd been
uneasy since the boys described the hole to him, and had come back to see
whether they'd actually returned, and to make sure of something at the
bottom of it that was his business alone (see §3 for what that something
might be — deliberately left open).

The boys reach the cave, light a torch, and climb onto the rocks to look
into the hole, exactly as the existing story already has them do. This is
where the stranger confronts them — not violently at first, but enough to
frighten them and draw them away from the edge. Tony, following at a
distance, sees a grown man laying hands on the two boys and closes the gap
fast. What happens next is a fight, not a plan: Tony goes for the
stranger, the stranger is armed (a knife, plausible enough for a man who
spends his time alone in the woods), and Andrew — trying to pull Arnie
clear — gets caught in it. The long cut down his leg comes from being
thrown against the rock; the near-severing of his hand comes from getting
it between himself and the blade while trying to shield his brother. Both
injuries, no single clean cause — which is exactly why "no explanation has
been established for the circumstances surrounding Andrew John Spencer's
injuries" in the official report (see §2).

Somewhere in the struggle, the cord or pocket carrying Tony's pendant —
his late father's, engraved on the back, the one thing of value he owned —
tears free and lands on the cave floor near the rocks. Arnie, shoved back
from the fight and looking for anything to hold onto, sees it catch the
torchlight and grabs it without thinking — the kind of thing a nine-year-
old does on pure instinct in the middle of chaos he doesn't understand.
An instant later, scrambling backward from the fight, he loses his
footing on the loose rock at the lip of the hole. He goes over. The
pendant is knocked from his hand as he grabs for the edge and comes to
rest on a ledge just below the rim — close enough for someone to
eventually find with a rope, far enough that nobody hears him land.

The stranger breaks off — whether hurt, or simply seizing the one moment
everyone's attention is on the hole instead of him — and disappears back
into the forest. He is never caught. He is never named in anything that
survives to the present day.

Andrew, in shock, runs. This matches the existing story exactly: he
reaches the farmyard alone, screaming for his parents, with no mention of
Tony anywhere near him. Tony doesn't go with him — he can't. He's left
standing at a hole he has no rope long enough to check, with a stranger
he can't chase and won't explain, having just watched a nine-year-old go
over the edge because of a fight he started by intervening. There's no
version of the truth he can tell that doesn't end with "I was here
illegally, looking for silver, when a boy died because of it." So he
says nothing. He waits, composes himself, and — as the household is
already gathering around Andrew — walks up "from the direction of the
outbuildings," "visibly confused by the noise and commotion," and stares
in horror at injuries he already knows the shape of. Every word of that
is already in the shipped story. It reads, on a second pass, as a
performance.

### Afterward

Andrew dies later on without finishing his account — he loses
consciousness mid-sentence in the existing text, which is also already
the reason nobody alive ever hears the whole thing from him. The August
search finds the boys' physical evidence (handkerchief, lunchbox, rope,
torch) but not the pendant — it's down the hole, underwater out of reach of a
surface sweep.

Lt. William McLeod, roping down into
the hole out of thoroughness nobody else bothered with, finds the
pendant. It goes into evidence. Nobody at the time connects the engraved
name on the back to tony, and the case is already going
cold; its ownership is never resolved, which is exactly what the existing
`williammcleodfindpendant` article already says: "the ownership remains
classified... due to the investigation officially still remaining open."

The search official winds down but, per the existing `thomasorourke`
record, continues for years afterward "in more of a 'casual weekend
divers' sort of way. 

Tony can't let it go. In 1902, when Thomas O'Rourke founds the Honey Dew
Caving Club, Tony joins — publicly a hobby, privately the only way he can
keep going back into that system looking for another way down to wherever
Arnie ended up. He's in the 1904 team photo as "Anthony Worthing." The
club's decade of mapping the Black Pine system, and their decision to hand
the finished map to police "in the hope it would one day help them target
certain areas in their search" (already in the `thomasorourke` record), is
Tony's real motive wearing the club's public one as cover, he needs to find
that pendant to point the evidence away from him, he doest know its already
found -- he saw Arnie pick it up and then go over the edge.

Henry "Harry" Whitmore, another club associate, hears the story of the
golden pendant the search turned up — secondhand, through the
caving circle — and it becomes one of the fifty entries in his 1907 book,
*Strange Things Found In Even Stranger Places*. He never learns the full truth.
Someone else, later, either does or fears he might: the library's copy of the book has
had that specific page torn out. Who did it, and when, is left open — but we know this is how Tony discovered the pendant had been found and was in police evidence.

## 2. How this sits under what's already written

None of this requires changing `story_en.md`, the missing person report,
or any existing web-content record. It only requires reading them as an
incomplete account rather than a false one — which is exactly what a
missing-person cold case *is*. Specifically:

- **"Where's Tony?" / "About somewhere."`** — already reads as Tony's
  absence being noticed and shrugged off. No change needed; it's the first
  clue in the existing text, unrecognised as one until now.
- **The unlocked tool shed** ("I thought this was always locked" / "They
  must have forgotten to lock it last night") — a small existing loose
  end, now explained as Tony's own late-night visit for rope and a knife.
- **The stranger on the path** — his insider knowledge of the hole, his
  unease, and his parting line about not having heard the mine mentioned
  "in years" already read as someone with unfinished business there. This
  proposal just gives him a reason to come back.
- **Andrew's injuries, "no explanation... established"** — the official
  record's honest gap, not a plot hole. The player now can explain it; the
  in-universe report never could, because nobody who could explain it
  ever spoke.
- **Andrew running home alone, and Tony's staged arrival "from the
  direction of the outbuildings," "visibly confused"** — already exactly
  as written. This is the detail that makes the whole thing work: reread
  after the fact, it's Tony performing innocence, not narrating it.
- **Four sets of footprints, two of them adult** (`mysteryoldnw`) —
  already established. They're Tony's and the stranger's.
- **The pendant recovered by McLeod, ownership left unresolved**
  (`williammcleodfindpendant`) — already established, and its vagueness
  about *when* during "the investigation" it was found is exactly what
  lets it be a later, informal find rather than part of the original
  sweep, with zero contradiction to the October report's evidence list.
- **The Honey Dew Caving Club's map handed to police "to target certain
  areas"** (`thomasorourke`) — already established, and already framed as
  more purposeful than casual hobbyists usually are.
- **The vandalised page in Harry's book, captioned as relating to "a
  golden pendant found by search party member Lieutenant William McLeod in
  1901"** (`strangethingsfoundinevenstrangerplaces` catalog entry) —
  already established, already pointed at exactly this pendant. In this
  version of the story it does double duty: it's also the moment, in 1907,
  that Tony first learns the pendant was recovered years earlier and has
  been sitting in police evidence the whole time — the piece of knowledge
  that gives him the strongest possible reason to want that page gone.
- **Simon Cresswell and Anthony Worthing both appearing in the 1904 club
  photo** — currently flagged in `story-timeline.md` §4 as an *unconfirmed*
  coincidence. This story would confirm it outright: Tony joined for the
  reason above, and it's an open question (§3) whether Simon ever joined
  knowing why.

Nothing above requires retconning a single sentence already shipped. It's
additive.

## 3. Open questions — deliberately left for you to decide

I've picked directions I think are strongest, but these are the load-
bearing choices left in your hands rather than mine:

1. **Who is the stranger / the man in the cave, really?** Recommended:
   leave him permanently unnamed — a missing-person mystery that produces
   *another*, smaller missing-person mystery, never closed, is a strong
   note to end on rather than a loose thread to feel bad about. Runner-up
   option: give him a name late, but only ever as a name — no face, no
   fate, so the player can't tidy him into a solved case.
2. **What was *he* protecting at the bottom of the hole?** Left
   deliberately blank in §1. Options, roughly ranked: (a) nothing
   supernatural, just something he did or hid decades earlier that he
   can't risk anyone finding; (b) tied to the "strange cries" folklore the
   stranger himself repeats — implying he isn't the first person to lose
   someone down there, which would be a genuinely dark echo of Arnie's own
   fate; (c) leave it wholly unanswered, a mystery inside the mystery. My
   preference is (b), used sparingly — it retroactively makes the
   stranger's own dialogue on Day 1 chilling rather than just ominous.
3. **Does Simon know?** Two clean options: he doesn't, and never has — a
   cleaner, sadder read where Tony carried this entirely alone. Or he
   suspects, was told, or figured it out much later — which sets up a
   second living witness with a different, softer or harder account of
   the same year, discoverable only very late. I'd lean toward "suspects
   but was never told outright" — enough for a late, ambiguous piece of
   testimony without turning Simon into a second confessor.
4. **Who tore the page out of Harry's book, and when?** This one now
   points more directly than before: §1 establishes that reading this
   exact page in 1907 is how Tony learned the pendant had already been
   found, which gives him the clearest possible motive and a specific
   date to have acted on it. Recommended: Tony himself, not long after
   1907 — quietly, maybe decades into his own search, on realising the
   thing he'd been diving for was already sitting in an evidence locker.
   It's a small, human, non-violent act next to everything else he's
   already done, which is exactly why it's a stronger character beat than
   leaving it to a stranger. Keep the *library's* framing unchanged
   though — their note still just asks the public who damaged it, so this
   stays something only the player pieces together, never confirmed
   in-universe.
5. **Is Arnie's body ever actually found?** The pendant itself isn't
   really an open question any more — §1 has Lt. McLeod recovering it in
   1901 and it's already sitting in evidence per the existing
   `williammcleodfindpendant` article, with only its *ownership* left
   unresolved. What stays open is Arnie himself. Recommended: no. The
   case stays formally open at the end of the game, same as it is today.
   The player solves *what happened*, not *where he is*, which fits a
   "bottomless" hole a lot better than a tidy recovery
   would.

## 4. New evidence to produce

Grouped in the order a player would plausibly uncover them, following the
same "flavour text supplies the next keyword" discoverability model
already documented in `happypath.md`. Each entry is a concept, not a spec —
exact wording, photo composition and search terms are yours to write; I'm
proposing what each piece needs to *do*, and roughly where it'd have to
sit to keep the existing clue chain intact.

### Tier 1 — the pendant gets a name

**✅ Implemented, in a merged form.** Items 1–3 below were the proposal;
what actually shipped is one Police Records document
(`fairchildinsurancerecordworthingpendant`, code `09-24-49-02-07`, Level 3)
that does the job of all three at once — an insurance valuation for the
pendant itself, naming **Percival Anthony Worthing** as the original
insured, with a later endorsement recording his death and the policy's
continuation by his son, **Anthony Worthing**. His death year is **1897**
(not 1895 as first drafted here — corrected to match the shipped record).
This also gives the `t.fairchild` credential, previously a dead end per
`happypath.md`, its purpose. Two things worth flagging about how it
actually shipped, versus the proposal:
- There's also now a *separate*, simpler Level 3 record (`goldenpendant`,
  keywords `pendant` / `gold pendant` / `p a worthing`) that awards the
  pendant itself as photo evidence, engraved "P. A. WORTHING." Both this
  and the fuller insurance/endorsement record at `09-24-49-02-07` are now
  legitimately reachable via clues already in the game (see
  `happypath.md` §8) — the Fairchild ZoomSearch page carries a standing
  line ("we're the lucky 7 when it comes to Insurance") that supplies
  the Route segment directly, closing what was briefly a gap. The decode
  book's own index tables also dropped their leading zeros since (`9`
  not `09`), and its Name Index was reshuffled so Fairchild lands on `7`
  — Hollingworth (`22`) and Worthing (`49`) kept their exact original
  numbers, so neither shipped code needed to change.
- The original three-item split proposed a separate death record and a
  separate "next of kin" record; the shipped version folds both into one
  endorsement line on the insurance record instead. Simpler, and it still
  does the job.

### Tier 2 — placing Tony at the scene, not just naming him

4. **✅ Implemented.** A close-up photo of the pendant itself, engraving
   visible, as physical evidence — this shipped as the `goldenpendant`
   Police record (Level 3, keywords `pendant` / `gold pendant` /
   `p a worthing`), which awards the **GOLD PENDANT** photo showing the
   "P. A. WORTHING" engraving. Legitimately reachable via clues already in
   the game — see `happypath.md` §8.
5. **A second, more complete account of Andrew's dying words** — a
   family diary page (Diane's, most plausibly) or a doctor's/coroner's
   private note not included in the official NWMP report, adding
   fragments like "a man," "a knife," "Tony" — enough to imply a third
   party without spelling out the whole sequence. This would sit as a
   report evidence item, since it's exactly the kind of thing a player
   finds "cleaning out an old room," consistent with how this game already
   frames its evidence discovery. - make andrew say he wants a private word.  He tells Diane some more secrets that we dont get to know til later and then dies alone at her side.

### Tier 3 — the struggle itself

6. **A piece of physical scene evidence suggesting a fight** — a torn
   scrap of clothing not matching either boy, or a second knife/blade
   found later near the rocks. This is what turns "two adults' footprints"
   from Hannah Fletcher's book into "two adults who fought," not just
   "two adults who happened to be nearby."

### Tier 4 — Tony's own long search

7. **An annotated page from the Honey Dew Caving Club's survey notes**,
   in Tony's hand, marking the hole's location with a private, repeated
   notation across several years ("checked again — nothing") that has
   nothing to do with mapping and everything to do with searching. This
   is the piece that reframes the club's decade of work as Tony's private
   obsession wearing a hobby's clothes. Per §1, the notation only makes
   sense for the years *before* 1907 — dated entries could be shown
   trailing off or changing tone around then, which is the moment Tony
   learns (via Harry's book, below) that there's nothing left down there
   to physically recover.

### Tier 5 — closing it, without fully closing it

8. **Tony's own unsent letter or private confession**, found late — among
   Honey Dew Club papers after his death, most plausibly — admitting he
   saw the pendant, saw Arnie pick it up, saw the fight that cost Andrew
   his injuries, and never told a soul that Arnie went into the hole
   rather than simply vanishing in the woods. Per §1, this would sit
   chronologically after 1907 — written once he already knew the pendant
   was in evidence and had, most likely, already torn the page out
   himself (see Open Question 4) — which is what makes a letter rather
   than a courtroom confession the right shape for it: by then there was
   nothing left to do with the truth but write it down. This is the
   capstone: the piece that turns everything above from a strong
   inference into an admission, without ever producing the stranger's
   name or Arnie's remains.

## 5. The Noticeboard payoff

`NOTICEBOARD_STATE` already exists as a scene the player can switch to
(`#noticeboardButton`, the corkboard in `index.html`) but currently has no
pin/connection mechanic built — it's an empty board. This story was
designed with that destination in mind, so whenever that feature gets
built, here's the shape a "solved" board could take:

**Pinned items, left to right, roughly in discovery order:**
missingReport → the four-footprints excerpt (`mysteryoldnw`) →
`williammcleodfindpendant` → the **GOLD PENDANT** photo (now real —
`goldenpendant`, Police Level 3) → the Fairchild insurance/endorsement
record (also real — `09-24-49-02-07`, Police Level 3, though see the
discoverability note under Tier 1) → the 1904 club photo, with Anthony
Worthing circled → Diane's diary page (item 5) → the torn clothing/second
blade (item 6) → Tony's annotated survey notes (item 7) → the vandalised
book page's catalog description → Tony's confession (item 8).

**String connections** the player would draw: pendant ↔ P.A. Worthing
(engraving); P.A. Worthing ↔ Anthony Worthing (father/son); Anthony
Worthing ↔ "Tony" (the farmhand, via the caving club record); Tony ↔ the
1901 cave (his confession); the stranger on the path ↔ the second set of
adult footprints (never a name, only an inference); Andrew's diary
fragment ↔ "a knife," "a man" (corroborating the footprints without
naming anyone); Tony's annotated survey notes trailing off ↔ the
vandalised book page (the notes stop searching right around when the book
told him there was nothing left in the cave to find); the vandalised book
page ↔ Tony's confession letter (motive and act, drawn as one line the
player has to infer rather than a fact stated anywhere in-universe).

**What the board deliberately never lets the player pin:** the stranger's
identity, and Arnie's location. Both stay open, by design (§3).
